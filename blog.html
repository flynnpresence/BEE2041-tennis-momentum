The Wrong Kind of Momentum

Why the type of pressure determines whether winning a big moment helps or hurts.

AuthorFlynn Presence
ModuleBEE2041 · University of Exeter

It is the fifth set of the 2023 Wimbledon final. Carlos Alcaraz, twenty years old, is serving for the biggest title of his life. Novak Djokovic, 23-time Grand Slam champion, stands across the net. The stadium is unanimous: something has shifted. The momentum, every pundit agrees, belongs to Alcaraz now.

He went on to win. The narrative wrote itself.

But did winning under that kind of pressure make the next points more likely to go his way? Or did fifteen thousand people in the stands fall for one of sport’s oldest statistical traps?

Gilovich et al. (1985) famously dismissed the “hot hand” (the feeling that one success magically increases the odds of the next) as a “cognitive illusion.” However, Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) proved this analysis contained a streak selection bias that systematically underestimated genuine persistence. In tennis, recent Cumulative Sum (CUSUM)-based studies (tracking cumulative performance deviations) like Du et al. (2025) find strong statistical evidence of momentum, but rely on thin 31-match samples measuring correlation, not cause.

This project scales the test. I analysed 56,253 points across all four 2023 Grand Slams and both tours, seven times the size of the most recent study. The results suggest momentum in tennis is context-dependent: the type of pressure determines whether winning a big point helps you or hurts you.

The raw data comes from Jeff Sackmann’s Match Charting Project, a volunteer-compiled database where tennis analysts manually record every point and exact score state. Marginal human input error is possible, though 56,253 observations substantially mitigate its impact. The same dataset has been used in prior momentum research, including Du et al. (2025); the contribution here is scale and the break point vs tiebreak distinction. This allowed precise identification of high-pressure situations: break points (where the returner is one point from winning the game) and tiebreaks (where a set hangs in the balance). I defined these as high-leverage moments: break points (13.5%) and tiebreak points (3.3%). Across the ATP (men’s) tour, 17% of points were high-leverage; across the WTA (women’s) tour, 16%.

The dataset covers 38,488 ATP points (169 matches) and 17,765 WTA points (128 matches), reflecting the men’s best-of-five format.

Data was standardised by matching player records and excluding retirements and qualifying rounds. Official ATP and WTA rankings measure baseline skill, consistent with Kovalchik (2016). Rankings were chosen over Elo (a dynamic skill-rating system that updates after every match) because a two-stage matching process, using tournament-specific rankings with season-median fallback, achieved full coverage across all 56,253 points.

I kept the tours separate, since pooling would have obscured the comparison that matters most.

Does Momentum Even Show Up in the Data?

Does winning a point make the next one more likely to go your way?

I ran two tests per player: a Pearson Chi-squared independence test and a Wald-Wolfowitz runs test. The Chi-squared test asks whether winning a point is completely independent of the previous point; the runs test asks whether players go on hot streaks more often than you’d expect from a coin flip. I tested 78 ATP and 78 WTA players separately; pooling would risk hiding individual patterns inside aggregate noise.

The results are the first blow to the momentum theory. In the ATP, 14.1% of players showed evidence of non-random sequences on the Chi-squared test, and 16.7% on the runs test. In the WTA, the figures are lower still: 5.1% and 6.4%. After applying a Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate (the proportion of flagged results that are actually false alarms) across all players, no ATP players survive correction on the Chi-squared test while two survive on the runs test; one WTA player survives on the Chi-squared test while none survive on the runs test. The uncorrected figures barely exceeded the 5% random baseline; once adjusted, evidence for individual momentum largely disappears.

But I cannot stop here. Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) proved traditional methods like the runs test are notoriously underpowered and systematically biased against finding momentum in short sequences.

Figure 2 tracks the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) score across the longest WTA match. Think of this as a running tally of overperformance. Every point is compared to the player’s match average: when they win points at a higher rate than usual, the line climbs. When they go cold, the line drops. Genuine momentum would look like a staircase, not a weather chart.

Figure 2 shows a sustained above-average run that eventually collapses. The line spends the first 150 points predominantly above zero, suggesting Alexandrova is controlling the match, before dropping sharply from around point 180 to its lowest point at 187. There is no sustained directional run anywhere. Du et al. (2025) argue this oscillation is itself evidence of momentum shifting between players. I read it differently: it looks like the natural variance of two evenly matched competitors trading points.

What About the Biggest Moments?

For every player, I calculated their Tiebreak Over-Expectation (TBOE). This metric asks a simple question: during tiebreaks, does the player perform above or below their running average for that specific match? If certain players consistently raise their game, I would expect outliers pulling sharply away from zero.

Figure 3 plots TBOE scores for players with at least 20 tiebreak points, from lowest to highest, to prevent finite-sample variance from creating artificial outliers (Miller and Sanjurjo, 2018). Of the full sample, 28 ATP players and 7 WTA players qualify. In the ATP, the distribution runs from Daniel Altmaier at -0.19 to Dennis Novak at +0.08, with no player pulling sharply away from the pack. The WTA sample is too small for strong conclusions: only 7 players qualify, and Yulia Putintseva at -0.27 sits visually apart, though this likely reflects the limited sample rather than genuine clutch ability.

TBOE captures an approximate within-match tiebreak tendency; it cannot reveal whether winning a specific pressure point causes the next one to go the same way.

Correlation or Causation?

Everything so far has tested whether momentum correlates with winning the next point. But correlation is not causation. A player already dominating will naturally win more break points. The association might simply reflect that good players win more of everything, not that winning one big point caused the next point.

I use a causal forest (an advanced machine learning model) to estimate whether winning a pressure point causes a higher probability of winning the next. Unlike standard methods that assume a one-size-fits-all rule, a causal forest estimates how momentum affects different subgroups of players without overfitting. The model relies on one crucial assumption: once we adjust for player skill, recent form, and match trajectory, there are no “hidden factors” secretly driving both the big pressure point and the point immediately after.

A high-leverage win is a break point or tiebreak point won by the player we are tracking, consistent with Gilovich et al. (1985) and Miller and Sanjurjo (2018), who define momentum as success breeding success. Restricting the control group to lost pressure points was rejected because, in tiebreaks, the alternating serve structure can mechanically disadvantage the point winner on the next point. The baseline therefore comprises all other match points.

Four variables are held constant: player ranking, rolling win percentage over the last ten points, winning streak length over the last four points, and the CUSUM match tracker introduced earlier. Window lengths of 10 and 4 balance responsiveness with stability; sensitivity was not formally tested.

To manage computation, the causal forest subsamples to 15,000 observations per run using stratified sampling; the ATP is subsampled from 38,488 points and the WTA from 17,765 points. CUSUM absorbs accumulated match-level drift. It is treated as a control for match drift, not momentum itself, so the treatment effect captures the incremental impact of winning a pressure point beyond existing match drift. A Bayesian prior (a starting estimate that updates as evidence accumulates) of 0.38 (the tour-wide return win rate) anchors initial expectations. Standard errors are clustered by match to account for intra-match dependence, and one player is randomly selected per match to avoid double-counting zero-sum points. A Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) check, which ensures our control variables aren’t overlapping and confusing the model, confirmed everything is mathematically stable (maximum score 1.22, well within safe bounds).

Figure 4 shows the causal effect by ranking band. In the ATP, the effect sits below zero across all four bands. In the WTA, top-ranked players show a negative effect, while lower-ranked players show a small positive effect, suggesting momentum may operate differently outside the elite.

Figure 5 breaks this down by variable. Two findings stand out. First, the break point win row sits left of zero in both tours, while the tiebreak win row sits right of zero in the ATP and left of zero in the WTA. Second, rolling win percentage runs in opposite directions between tours: negative for the ATP and positive for the WTA, though the WTA error bar is wide, making the estimate imprecise. This suggests recent form plays a different role across tours, a nuance that would be invisible if the datasets had been merged. Player rank and the other control rows sit close to zero across both tours, suggesting baseline skill alone does not determine who wins the next point.

The combined causal effect is negative for both tours (ATP: -0.0317, WTA: -0.0234). In the ATP, the combined estimate is pulled toward zero by the opposing tiebreak effect: the aggregate (-0.0317) is less negative than the break point effect alone (-0.0449), masking a meaningful divergence between the two pressure types. Pooling all high-leverage moments creates a misleading aggregate that buries the directional difference.

If I stopped here, the hot hand would look like a complete fallacy. But this combined number treats all high-leverage moments as identical. What happens if I split the two types of pressure apart?

The Finding That Changes Everything

As Figure 6 shows, converting a break point actually causes a drop in the probability of winning the next point across both tours. Break points generate a clear, measurable drag. Think of it as an emotional hangover: perhaps the relief of winning a crucial point briefly lowers focus or intensity for the point that follows.

Tiebreaks tell a more complex story that splits by tour. In the ATP, winning a tiebreak point generates a genuine positive causal effect (+0.0153): the kind of momentum crowds imagine. In the WTA, the effect reverses sharply. Winning a tiebreak point is estimated to reduce the probability of winning the next one by -0.0887 — roughly twice as large in magnitude as the ATP break point hangover. The WTA tiebreak result is based on 207 treated observations, a smaller sample than the break point analyses, and the precise magnitude should be treated with caution; however, the direction is clear and the estimate is tightly constrained (SE = 0.0057, 95% CI: −0.100 to −0.078).

This tour-specific split explains why the combined momentum number in the previous section was negative. In the ATP, break points are far more common than tiebreaks — 13.5% of all ATP points are break points, compared to just 3.8% for tiebreaks — so the hangover effect dominates the aggregate, burying the positive tiebreak signal. In the WTA, both effects run in the same direction; there is no cancellation, only compounding.

If Not Momentum, Then What?

Outside of tiebreaks, if a short-term momentum boost does not drive point outcomes, what does? Figure 7 ranks the four control variables by their contribution to the model’s estimates.

The cumulative match tracker (the CUSUM metric introduced earlier) remains the dominant variable, though its lead differs by tour. In the WTA, accumulated match drift accounts for 53% of the total, comfortably ahead of player rank (27%) and rolling win percentage (18%). In the ATP, CUSUM leads at 46%, but rolling win percentage has risen sharply to 25% — nearly as influential as player rank (27%). Together, rank and recent form account for over half of the ATP model’s predictive weight.

Winning Streak, which measures whether a player won their last four consecutive points, scores just 2% in both tours. It is virtually flat. Knowing that a player is on a short hot streak tells the model almost nothing once the overarching match drift is accounted for. The hot hand, in its most literal form, has no predictive power here.

What shapes the outcome of these high-leverage moments most is accumulated match drift, not short-term bursts of form.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

Within this sample, momentum is not universal: it is context-dependent across both pressure type and tour. Winning a break point is estimated to reduce the probability of winning the next point by around 4-5 percentage points in the ATP and 1 in the WTA — a consistent hangover across both tours, the exact opposite of what commentators assume. The tiebreak story diverges. In the ATP, winning a tiebreak point generates positive momentum (+0.0153), consistent with the crowd’s intuition. In the WTA, the effect is sharply negative (-0.0887), larger in magnitude than the break point drag. The WTA tiebreak estimate rests on 207 treated observations, so the precise figure is provisional, but the direction is robust. In the ATP, the opposing tiebreak and break point effects partially cancel in aggregate analyses that treat all pressure moments as identical.

The core findings are mathematically stable. The break point hangover holds in both tours under both model specifications. The ATP tiebreak positive effect is directionally consistent under the reduced control set. The WTA tiebreak negative direction is also consistent, though its magnitude is less stable given the small treated sample.

In fact, the break point “hangover” is even more severe than it looks. Converting a break point transitions the winner to serving, which carries a structural serving advantage. The negative hangover effect persists despite this tailwind, suggesting a causal drag strong enough to outweigh the structural advantage of the serve.

This analysis has real boundaries. Estimating all effects within a single model mitigates the risk of p-hacking, but volunteer-charted datasets naturally skew toward elite, televised matches, and this data cannot measure unobserved physiological factors like fatigue. This model only tests immediate point-to-point effects; whether momentum compounds across longer sequences remains an open question for future research.

Ultimately, the picture is more nuanced than prior research suggested. In tennis, researchers have long debated whether the hot hand exists. The evidence here suggests they may have been asking too broad a question. The break point hangover is real and consistent across both tours. ATP tiebreak momentum is real. Whether WTA tiebreaks genuinely generate a large negative effect, or whether 207 observations is insufficient to pin down the true magnitude, is a question this dataset cannot fully resolve. And because break points occur roughly four times more often than tiebreaks, any pooled analysis will be dominated by the hangover signal, regardless of what is happening in tiebreaks.

The next time a commentator says Carlos Alcaraz has the momentum after converting a break point, the data suggests caution. If he just won a tiebreak point, they might actually be onto something.

Data · Jeff Sackmann Match Charting Project and ATP/WTA official results (2023).
