The Wrong Kind of Momentum

Why the type of pressure determines whether winning a big moment helps or hurts.

AuthorFlynn Presence
ModuleBEE2041 · University of Exeter

It is the fifth set of the 2023 Wimbledon final. Carlos Alcaraz, twenty years old, is serving for the biggest title of his life. Novak Djokovic, 23-time Grand Slam champion, stands across the net. The stadium is unanimous: something has shifted. The momentum, every pundit agrees, belongs to Alcaraz now.

He went on to win. The narrative wrote itself.

But did winning under that kind of pressure make the next points more likely to go his way? Or did fifteen thousand people in the stands fall for one of sport’s oldest statistical traps?

Gilovich et al. (1985) famously dismissed the “hot hand” (the belief that a player’s chance of success is greater following a previous success than following a failure) as a “cognitive illusion.” However, Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) proved this analysis contained a streak selection bias. In tennis, recent Cumulative Sum (CUSUM)-based studies like Du et al. (2025) find strong statistical evidence of momentum, but rely on thin 31-match samples measuring correlation, not cause.

This project scales the test. I analysed 56,253 points from volunteer-charted matches across all four 2023 Grand Slams and both tours, seven times the size of the most recent study. The results suggest that what commentators call momentum after a break point is real but it has nothing to do with momentum.

The raw data comes from Jeff Sackmann’s Match Charting Project, a volunteer-compiled database where tennis analysts manually record every point and exact score state. The same dataset has been used in prior momentum research, including Du et al. (2025); the contribution here is scale and the break point vs tiebreak distinction. Break points and tiebreaks are the high-leverage moments: genuinely rare, making up roughly 12% of ATP points and 13% of WTA points.

The dataset covers 38,488 ATP points (169 matches, best-of-five sets) and 17,765 WTA points (128 matches, best-of-three). Official rankings control for baseline skill. I kept the tours separate, since pooling would have obscured the comparison that matters most.

Does Momentum Even Show Up in the Data?

Does winning a point make the next one more likely to go your way?

I ran two tests per player: a Chi-squared independence test and a Wald-Wolfowitz runs test. The Chi-squared test asks whether winning a point is completely independent of the previous point; the runs test asks whether players go on hot streaks more often than you’d expect from a coin flip. I tested 78 ATP and 78 WTA players separately; pooling would risk hiding individual patterns inside aggregate noise.

The results are the first blow to the momentum theory. In the ATP, 14.1% of players showed evidence of non-random sequences on the Chi-squared test, and 15.4% on the runs test. In the WTA, the figures are lower still: 5.1% and 7.7%. After applying a Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate (the proportion of flagged results that are actually false alarms) across all players, no ATP players survive correction on the Chi-squared test while one survives on the runs test; one WTA player survives on the Chi-squared test and one survives on the runs test. In the WTA, the uncorrected figures barely exceeded the 5% random baseline; in the ATP, they ran higher but still fell well short of systematic evidence. Once adjusted, evidence for individual momentum largely disappears.

But I cannot stop here. Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) proved traditional methods like the runs test are notoriously underpowered and systematically biased against finding momentum in short sequences.

Figure 2 tracks the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) score across the longest WTA match. Think of this as a running tally of overperformance. Every point is compared to the player’s match average: when they win points at a higher rate than usual, the line climbs. When they go cold, the line drops. Genuine momentum would look like a staircase, not a weather chart.

Figure 2 shows Alexandrova performing above her own match average for almost the entire match. The line stays above zero for the first 88 points, dips briefly, reaching approximately −1.8 around point 100, then recovers sharply and climbs to finish above +4. Du et al. (2025) would interpret this oscillation as momentum shifting between players. But the chart tells a simpler story: one player performing consistently above her own average, interrupted by a single 15-point spell of below-average play that reverses immediately. That is not momentum. It is variance.

What About the Biggest Moments?

For every player, I calculated their Tiebreak Over-Expectation (TBOE). This metric asks whether, during tiebreaks, a player performs above or below their own match average. If certain players consistently raise their game, I would expect outliers pulling sharply away from zero.

Figure 3 plots TBOE scores for players with at least 20 tiebreak points, from lowest to highest, to prevent finite-sample variance from creating artificial outliers (Miller and Sanjurjo, 2018). Of the full sample, 28 ATP players and 7 WTA players qualify. In the ATP, the distribution runs from Daniel Altmaier at -0.14 to Mackenzie McDonald at +0.11, with no player pulling sharply away from the pack. The WTA sample is too small for strong conclusions: only 7 players qualify, and Yulia Putintseva at -0.27 sits visually apart at the bottom while Karolina Muchova leads at +0.12, though the spread likely reflects the limited sample rather than genuine clutch ability.

TBOE captures an approximate within-match tiebreak tendency; it cannot reveal whether winning a specific pressure point causes the next one to go the same way.

Correlation or Causation?

Everything so far has tested whether momentum correlates with winning the next point. But correlation is not causation. A player already dominating will naturally win more break points. The association might simply reflect that good players win more of everything, not that winning one big point caused the next point.

I use a causal forest (an advanced machine learning model) to estimate whether winning a pressure point causes a higher probability of winning the next. Unlike standard methods that assume a one-size-fits-all rule, a causal forest estimates the causal effect of winning a high-leverage point for different subgroups of players without overfitting. The model relies on one crucial assumption: once we adjust for player skill, recent form, and match trajectory, there are no “hidden factors” secretly driving both the big pressure point and the point immediately after.

A high-leverage win is a break point or tiebreak point won by the player we are tracking, consistent with the sequential dependence framework of Gilovich et al. (1985) and Miller and Sanjurjo (2018). Restricting the control group to lost pressure points was rejected because, in tiebreaks, the alternating serve structure can mechanically disadvantage the point winner on the next point. The baseline therefore comprises all other match points.

Four variables are held constant: player ranking, rolling win percentage over the last ten points, winning streak length over the last four points, and the CUSUM match tracker introduced earlier. Window lengths of 10 and 4 balance responsiveness with stability; sensitivity was not formally tested.

To manage computation, the causal forest subsamples to 15,000 observations per run using stratified sampling (ensuring treated and control observations are proportionally represented); the ATP is subsampled from 38,488 points and the WTA from 17,765 points. CUSUM absorbs accumulated match-level drift. It is treated as a control for match drift, not momentum itself, so the treatment effect captures the incremental impact of winning a pressure point beyond existing match drift. A Bayesian prior (a starting estimate that updates as evidence accumulates) of 0.38 (the tour-wide return win rate; returners win roughly 38 in every 100 points served) anchors initial expectations.

Standard errors for logistic estimates are clustered by match; causal forest standard errors reflect the standard error of the mean CATE (the individual causal effect estimated for each observation) across observations. One player is randomly selected per match to avoid double-counting zero-sum points. A Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) check, which ensures our control variables are not overlapping and confusing the model, confirmed everything is mathematically stable (maximum score 1.22; VIF above 10 indicates problematic overlap, so 1.22 is extremely low).

Figure 4 shows the causal effect by ranking band. In the ATP, the combined effect sits above zero across all four ranking bands; lower-ranked players (200+) show the largest estimated effect, though wider error bars signal greater uncertainty. In the WTA, the effect is smaller and more variable across bands, though all four bands remain above zero.

Figure 5 breaks this down by variable. Two findings stand out. First, the break point win row sits right of zero in both tours, while the tiebreak win row sits near zero in the ATP and left of zero in the WTA. Second, rolling win percentage runs in opposite directions between tours: negative for the ATP and positive for the WTA, though the WTA error bar is wide, making the estimate imprecise. This suggests recent form plays a different role across tours, a nuance that would be invisible if the datasets had been merged. Player rank and the other control rows sit close to zero across both tours, suggesting baseline skill alone does not determine who wins the next point.

The combined causal effect is positive for both tours (ATP: +0.0957, WTA: +0.0532). In the ATP, the combined estimate sits below the break point effect alone (+0.1526) because the near-zero tiebreak result pulls the aggregate down. In the WTA, the combined figure (+0.0532) is below the break point effect (+0.0648) because the negative tiebreak result partially offsets it. Pooling all high-leverage moments into a single number obscures the divergence between the two pressure types.

If I stopped here, the picture would suggest that winning any high-leverage moment is simply good: a consistent positive boost. But this combined number treats break points and tiebreaks as identical. What happens when we split them apart?

The Finding That Changes Everything

As Figure 6 shows, winning a break point is estimated to boost the probability of winning the next point by around 15 percentage points in the ATP (95% CI: +0.150 to +0.155) and 6 percentage points in the WTA (95% CI: +0.062 to +0.067). But this positive signal is not evidence of psychological momentum. It reflects a built-in feature of how tennis scoring works: winning a break point always determines who serves the very next point, not approximately but exactly. When a server saves a break point, no game has ended; the current game continues and the server keeps serving the next point. When a returner converts a break point, the game ends immediately and the next point is the first of the following game, which falls to the former returner now serving. In both cases, the focal player who won the break point serves the very next point with certainty, verified empirically across 1,496 ATP break point wins (100%). The serving advantage alone accounts for most of this effect: servers win approximately 60–65% of points, which explains the majority of the 15 percentage point boost.

Tiebreaks tell a different story, precisely because that scoring mechanism is absent. In a tiebreak, serve alternates every two points rather than flipping between entire games, so winning one tiebreak point does not confer a clear serving advantage on the next. In the ATP, winning a tiebreak point generates a near-zero causal effect (+0.0003, SE = 0.0016, 95% CI: −0.003 to +0.003): statistically indistinguishable from zero. In the WTA, the effect is sharply negative. Winning a tiebreak point is estimated to reduce the probability of winning the next one by -0.0564, a direction that runs opposite to the break point result in the same tour. The WTA tiebreak result is based on 209 treated observations, a smaller sample than the break point analyses, and the precise magnitude should be treated with caution; however, the confidence interval excludes zero entirely (95% CI: −0.073 to −0.040).

This tour-specific split explains the combined estimates. In the ATP, break points are more common than tiebreaks (8.1% of all ATP points compared to 3.8%), so the positive break point effect dominates the combined figure, pulling it above zero despite the null tiebreak result. In the WTA, the two effects run in opposite directions: the positive break point effect is partially offset by the negative tiebreak result, producing a smaller combined positive.

If Not Momentum, Then What?

Break point wins are dominated by a structural mechanism: the serving transition that follows. The tiebreak strips that mechanism away. What drives outcomes across both pressure types? Figure 7 ranks the four control variables by their contribution to the model’s estimates.

The cumulative match tracker (the CUSUM metric introduced earlier) is the dominant variable. In both tours, CUSUM leads by a wide margin. In the WTA, accumulated match drift accounts for 56% of the total, ahead of player rank (25%) and rolling win percentage (16%). In the ATP, CUSUM also leads at 56%, followed by player rank at 23% and rolling win percentage at 20%. Together, rank and recent form account for around 43% of the ATP model’s predictive weight.

Winning Streak, which measures whether a player won their last four consecutive points, scores 1% in the ATP and 2% in the WTA. It is virtually flat. Knowing that a player is on a short hot streak tells the model almost nothing once the overarching match drift is accounted for. The hot hand, in its most literal form, has no predictive power here.

What most determines how winning a high-leverage point affects a player is accumulated match drift, not short-term bursts of form.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

Within this sample, momentum is context-dependent, and the context that matters most is whether the pressure point changes who serves next. Winning a break point is estimated to boost the probability of winning the next point by around 15 percentage points in the ATP and 6 in the WTA. But this is a structural effect, not a psychological one: winning a break point always determines who serves the very next point, the winner keeps or gains the serve with certainty, and that mechanical advantage drives most of the estimate. Tiebreaks, where serve alternates every two points, are the cleaner test of pure momentum, and there the story is different. In the ATP, winning a tiebreak point generates a null effect (+0.0003, SE = 0.0016, 95% CI: −0.003 to +0.003), statistically indistinguishable from zero. In the WTA, the effect is sharply negative (-0.0564), running opposite to the break point direction in the same tour. The WTA tiebreak estimate rests on 209 treated observations, so the precise figure is provisional, but the direction is robust.

The core findings are mathematically stable. The findings hold across both model specifications (ATP combined: +0.0957 full controls, +0.1070 ranking-only; WTA combined: +0.0532 full controls, +0.0603 ranking-only). The robustness check was run on the combined treatment only; individual pressure-type effects were not separately retested under reduced controls.

This analysis has real boundaries: estimating all effects within a single model mitigates the risk of p-hacking, but volunteer-charted datasets naturally skew toward elite, televised matches, and this data cannot measure unobserved physiological factors like fatigue. This model only tests immediate point-to-point effects; whether momentum compounds across longer sequences remains an open question for future research. The break point estimate reflects the total effect of winning a break point, including the serving transition that follows. Isolating the purely psychological component (separating break saves from break conversions, and controlling for the subsequent serving position) would require subgroup analysis beyond the scope of this study.

Ultimately, the picture is more nuanced than prior research suggested. In tennis, researchers have long debated whether the hot hand exists. The evidence here suggests they may have been asking an imprecise question. The apparent positive effect of winning a break point is real and consistent, but it is structural, not psychological. When the structural mechanism is removed (as it largely is in tiebreaks), the signal disappears in the ATP and reverses in the WTA. Whether WTA tiebreaks genuinely generate a large negative effect, or whether 209 observations is insufficient to pin down the true magnitude, is a question this dataset cannot fully resolve. Because break points occur far more often than tiebreaks, any pooled analysis will be dominated by the serving-transition signal, masking the moments where psychological momentum would be most detectable.

The next time a commentator says Carlos Alcaraz has the momentum after converting a break point, the data offers a different reading: what he has is the serve, not psychological momentum. And if he just won a tiebreak point instead (the situation where the structural advantage is absent)? The ATP data finds no signal in either direction. That, not the break point, is where the question of psychological momentum is most cleanly put and most cleanly answered.
