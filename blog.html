The Wrong Kind of Momentum

Why break points kill momentum and tiebreaks create it.

AuthorFlynn Presence
ModuleBEE2041 · University of Exeter

It is the fifth set of the 2023 Wimbledon final. Carlos Alcaraz, twenty years old, is serving for the biggest title of his life. Novak Djokovic, 23-time Grand Slam champion, stands across the net. The stadium is unanimous: something has shifted. The momentum, every pundit agrees, belongs to Alcaraz now.

He went on to win. The narrative wrote itself.

But did winning under that kind of pressure actually make the next points more likely to go his way? Or did fifteen thousand people in the stands just fall for one of sport’s oldest statistical traps?

Gilovich et al. (1985) famously dismissed the “hot hand” as a cognitive illusion. However, Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) later proved this analysis contained a streak selection bias that systematically underestimated genuine persistence. In tennis, recent CUSUM-based studies like Du et al. (2025) find traces of momentum, but rely on thin 31-match samples measuring correlation, not cause.

This project scales the test entirely. I analyzed 52,314 points across all four 2023 Grand Slams on both the ATP and WTA tours, seven times the size of the most recent study. The findings transcend a simple yes or no.

The raw data comes from Jeff Sackmann’s Match Charting Project, a publicly available volunteer-compiled database where tennis analysts manually record every point played in professional matches, including the exact score state at each moment. As a volunteer-compiled database, marginal human input error is possible, though the scale of 52,314 observations substantially mitigates the impact of random data-entry errors. The same dataset has been used in prior momentum research, including Du et al. (2025); the contribution here is scale and the break point vs tiebreak distinction. This allowed me to identify the highest-pressure situations in the game with precision: break points, where the returner stands one point away from winning the service game, and tiebreak points, where an entire set hangs in the balance. I defined these as high-leverage moments, a category combining break points (13.5% of all points) and tiebreak points (3.5%). Across the ATP tour, 17% of points were high-leverage; across the WTA, 16%.

The dataset covers 35,635 ATP points (155 matches) and 16,679 WTA points (120 matches), the volume difference reflecting the men’s best-of-five format.

The raw data required substantial preparation. I matched player names across separate datasets, removed retirements, and standardised score formats across tens of thousands of individual points. I also filtered out qualifying rounds, as they feature lower-ranked players competing under entirely different conditions. I drew on official ATP and WTA rankings to measure each player’s baseline skill level, consistent with Kovalchik (2016), who found player ranking among the strongest baseline predictors of match outcomes. Official rankings were chosen over Elo ratings on practical grounds: a two-stage matching process, using tournament-specific rankings where available and season-average rankings as a fallback, achieved full coverage across all 52,314 points.

I kept the ATP and WTA tours completely separate throughout. What is true for men’s tennis is not necessarily true for women’s tennis, and pooling them would have obscured the comparison that turns out to matter most.

Does Momentum Even Show Up in the Data?

Does winning a point make the next one more likely to go your way?

I ran two tests for every player individually: a Pearson Chi-squared independence test and a Wald-Wolfowitz runs test. The Chi-squared test asks whether point outcomes are independent; the runs test asks whether wins and losses cluster beyond chance. I tested 77 ATP players and 78 WTA players separately, rather than pooling everyone together. Pooling would risk hiding individual patterns inside aggregate noise.

The results are the first blow to the momentum theory. In the ATP, 10.4% of players showed statistical evidence that their point sequences were non-random on the Chi-squared test, and 13.0% on the runs test. In the WTA, the figures are lower still: 6.4% and 9.0% respectively. After applying a Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate (the proportion of flagged results that are actually false alarms) across all players tested simultaneously, zero players remain significant on the Chi-squared test in either tour, and only one player per tour survives on the runs test. The uncorrected figures already barely exceeded the 5% random baseline; once properly adjusted, the statistically significant evidence for individual momentum vanishes.

But I cannot stop here. As Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) mathematically proved, traditional methods like the runs test are notoriously underpowered, meaning they will often miss real effects that genuinely exist, and systematically biased against finding momentum in short sequences.

The chart below tracks the cumulative momentum score across the longest WTA match in the dataset. The line rises when the player wins above her average rate and falls when she drops below. Genuine momentum would look like a staircase, not a weather chart.

What the chart actually shows is a sustained above-average run that eventually collapses. The line spends the first 150 points predominantly above zero, suggesting Alexandrova is controlling the match, before oscillating around the baseline. It then drops sharply from around point 180, reaching its lowest point at 187 before recovering. There is no sustained directional run anywhere. Du et al. (2025) argue that this kind of oscillation is itself evidence of momentum shifting between players. I read it differently: it looks like the natural variance of two evenly matched competitors trading points.

What About the Biggest Moments?

For every player, I calculated their Tiebreak Over-Expectation (TBOE): their actual tiebreak win rate minus their predicted win rate based on official ranking and recent form. If certain players consistently raise their game when it matters most, I would expect to see clear outliers pulling sharply away from zero in either direction.

The chart plots every player’s TBOE score from lowest to highest. In both the ATP and WTA, the distribution is smooth and continuous, running gradually from around -0.12 at the low end to +0.17 at the high end in the ATP, and from -0.20 to +0.25 in the WTA. There are no dramatic outliers. No player separates themselves convincingly from the pack as a consistent clutch performer.

Correlation or Causation?

Everything so far has tested whether momentum correlates with winning the next point. But correlation is not causation. A player who is already dominating will naturally win more break points. The association might simply reflect that good players win more of everything, not that winning one big point is itself responsible for winning the next.

I use a causal forest to estimate whether winning a pressure point causes a higher probability of winning the next, not just correlates with it. Think of it as a more rigorous version of asking “what would have happened otherwise.” Standard regression assumes the effect of winning a pressure point is identical for every player in every situation. The causal forest drops that assumption. It splits the data into subgroups, estimates the effect separately within each one, and uses separate halves of the data to draw the subgroups and calculate the effects, preventing it from finding patterns that are not really there. The key assumption is that controlling for the four control variables captures all systematic differences between players who win and lose high-leverage points, with no hidden factor is simultaneously driving the pressure outcome and the next point.

I define a high-leverage win as a point where the player faced a break point or tiebreak situation and won it. This follows directly from the academic literature. Gilovich et al. (1985) and Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) both define momentum as success breeding success, not merely exposure to pressure. Restricting the control group to lost pressure points alone was considered but rejected: in tiebreaks, the alternating serve structure often mechanically disadvantages the point winner by placing them in a receiving position on the following point, which would mask genuine momentum effects behind the rules of the sport, meaning the baseline for comparison comprises all other points played in the match.

The model holds four variables constant to isolate the pressure point effect: player ranking, rolling win percentage over the last ten points, winning streak length over the last four points, and cumulative momentum score. A Bayesian prior of 0.38 (the tour-wide return win rate) anchors initial break point expectations before updating on match evidence. Standard errors are clustered by match, since points within the same match are not independent. To avoid double-counting zero-sum points, one focal player is randomly selected per match and all observations tracked from their perspective.

The first chart shows the estimated causal effect broken down by player ranking band. Across all four bands in both tours, the effect sits below zero. Top-ranked players show no meaningful positive momentum effect. Neither do qualifiers.

The second chart breaks this down by variable. Two findings stand out. First, the pressure point causal effect sits just below zero in both tours, with tight error bars (error bars show the range of plausible values; tight bars mean a precise estimate). Second, rolling win percentage behaves in completely opposite directions between tours: negative for ATP, strongly positive for WTA, with the WTA error bar barely crossing zero, meaning the effect may be statistically indistinguishable from no effect at all. This suggests recent form plays a meaningfully different role in women’s tennis than in men’s, a tour-specific pattern that would have been invisible if the two datasets had been merged.

The combined causal effect is negative for both tours (ATP: -0.0325, WTA: -0.0363). Note that the combined WTA estimate is more negative than the break point estimate alone. This reflects Simpson’s Paradox (where pooling subgroups can contradict the subgroup results): the combined model is estimated on a different mix of players and situations than the sub-group models, and the composition of the treatment group shifts when break points and tiebreaks are pooled together.

If I stopped here, the universal hot hand would look like a fallacy. But remember, this combined number treats all high-leverage moments as identical. What happens if I split the two types of pressure apart?

The Finding That Changes Everything

As the chart shows, converting a break point reduces the probability of winning the next point in both tours. Break points generate a clear, measurable drag. Tiebreaks tell the opposite story. The ATP estimate (+0.0564) is the chart’s dominant feature, more than twice the magnitude of the break point hangover. The WTA effect (+0.0209) is consistent in direction but smaller, reflecting a limited 197-observation sample.

This also explains why the combined number is negative. Break points are far more common than tiebreaks: 13.5% of all ATP points are break points, compared to just 3.5% in tiebreaks. The hangover effect happens more often, so it dominates the aggregate. When all high-leverage moments are treated as identical, the tiebreak momentum signal gets buried.

If Not Momentum, Then What?

Outside of tiebreaks, if momentum does not drive point outcomes, what does? The chart below ranks the four variables by how much each one drives variation in the causal effect.

The cumulative momentum score dominates everything else. In the ATP it accounts for 0.50 of the variance in the treatment effect (the causal impact of winning a high-leverage point), significantly ahead of player rank at 0.32 and rolling win percentage at 0.16. In the WTA the pattern holds: momentum score at 0.52, player rank at 0.28, rolling win percentage at 0.19.

The most striking feature of the chart is the bar you can barely see. Winning Streak, which measures whether a player won their last four consecutive points, scores 0.024 in the ATP and 0.010 in the WTA. It is virtually flat. Knowing that a player is on a short hot streak tells the model almost nothing once the cumulative trajectory is already accounted for. The hot hand, in its most literal form, has no predictive power here.

What shapes the momentum effect most is accumulated match drift, not short-term bursts of form.

The Uncomfortable Conclusion

Within this sample, momentum in tennis is not universal. It operates in opposite directions depending on context. Winning a break point reduces the probability of winning the next point. Winning a tiebreak point increases it. The break point effect is a hangover, the opposite of what every commentator assumes. The tiebreak effect is genuine positive momentum, exactly what crowds expect, but only in that specific context. The evidence for these two effects is striking, opposite in sign, and has been cancelling each other out in every aggregate analysis of tennis that treated all pressure moments as identical. The finding holds up. Whether I control only for player ranking or include the full set of form variables, the results are consistent. The split between break points and tiebreaks is not an artefact of including too many control variables, which can sometimes manufacture artificial effects. It is stable.

I should note several limitations. Volunteer-charted datasets skew toward elite broadcast matches, as untelevised courts cannot be recorded. I have no physiological data, and winning a high-leverage point may reflect an unobserved factor affecting both points simultaneously. Standard errors are sampling-based estimates, and conclusions rest on consistency across model configurations rather than any single interval. I only test immediate point-to-point effects. Notably, converting a break point transitions the winner to serving, a position carrying a structural 64% point-win advantage. That the hangover effect persists despite this mechanical tailwind suggests the psychological drag is stronger than the serve advantage alone. Future research might employ Monte Carlo simulations of rank-weighted random sequences to establish whether the CUSUM oscillations fall within the bounds of natural variance.

But the picture is clearer than it has ever been at this scale. In tennis, researchers have long debated whether the hot hand exists. The evidence here suggests they may have been asking too broad a question. Momentum appears to exist in tiebreaks. It runs in reverse at break points. And because break points happen roughly four times more often than tiebreaks at Grand Slams, the positive effect has always been buried under the negative one.

The next time a commentator says Alcaraz has the momentum after converting a break point, the data suggests caution. If he just won a tiebreak point, they might actually be onto something.
